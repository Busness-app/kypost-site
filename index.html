Privacy by default

End-to-end means your server can't read it either

KyPost keeps your private key on your own machines instead of on the server — and everywhere encryption would quietly weaken, it stops and asks first.

01 — Key custody

Your key stays yours

Your private key is wrapped under your password and unwrapped only in your browser — the server can't decrypt your mail, and it no longer caches decrypted bodies to disk.

02 — Device enrollment

Encrypted mail reads on the phone now

Webmail seals your key to a key the device generated and never exported — Android's Keystore, Apple's Secure Enclave, or your own GnuPG keyring on Linux. Both screens show the same 14-character code first, and the browser refuses to seal if they differ, because the server is the party that could swap that key.

03 — Finding keys

Encryption without the key-signing party

Your server looks recipients up through Web Key Directory, then keyservers, and harvests Autocrypt headers from DKIM-authenticated mail — pinning what it finds on first use. Each rung is yours to switch off, any discovered key is yours to reject, and scanning a fingerprint in person is still there when it matters.

04 — Publishing

Be findable on your own domain

Verify a domain once by DNS, and your server serves WKD for it — so anyone in Thunderbird, GnuPG, or ProtonMail can encrypt to you unprompted. Publication is opt-out per user and only ever for addresses you actually send from.

05 — No silent downgrade

It refuses rather than weakens

An encrypted send to someone with no key on file is refused, not quietly turned into something weaker. Confirm the fallback and you get a one-time pickup link — sealed in your own browser, so your server stores only ciphertext it can't open.

06 — Signatures

Six answers, not a green tick

A signature badge is decided on your device, from keys your device already holds — the relay's own verdict is never read. Confirmed in person and same key as last time are different claims, so KyPost makes them different badges, and a key that changed under a sender you know raises an alarm instead of quietly re-pinning.

07 — Metadata

The subject line is inside the envelope

Encrypted mail carries its real subject inside the encrypted part using the LAMPS protected-headers convention, so the outer envelope says nothing. Push notifications are generic by default — sender and subject only travel to Google or Apple if you turn previews on.

08 — Under the hood

Your server, your rules

Mail lives on infrastructure you control, and unread-mail classification runs on a local Ollama model — no cloud AI provider ever sees your inbox.

Hardened by design

Encryption is the start, not the finish

Beyond PGP, every client ships device-level protections and the server guards its own front door — because a compromised device, a convincing email, or a patient guesser shouldn't mean a compromised inbox.

App lock

A PIN with escalating delays and wipe-on-repeat protects Android and Linux; Face ID, Touch ID, or a passcode protects Apple devices.

Hostile location mode

One tap disables on-device caching of mail, contacts, and attachments for border crossings or other high-risk situations.

Certificate pinning

Pairing pins your server's certificate, and later changes fail closed instead of silently trusting a hostile network. Where your server terminates TLS itself, the pin rides along in the pairing QR — so even the very first call is pinned rather than trusted on faith.

Encrypted at rest

Cached mail, contacts, and their PGP keys sit in a SQLCipher database on Android and Linux, keyed from the OS keystore. An existing plaintext profile converts itself on the next launch, and never deletes the original before the replacement verifies.

Credentials per device

Pairing mints an id and secret for that one device, and it can't sign in to webmail. Lose a phone and you revoke that phone — every other device keeps working.

Locked credentials

Seal relay credentials behind your PIN so background mail checks and MFA approvals wait until you unlock the app.

KyPost impersonation

The server flags mail impersonating KyPost, and clients reject kypost:// links before they can open a fraudulent pairing prompt.

Sandboxed messages

Sender HTML runs without JavaScript, remote content, or navigation. Blocked links are clearly labeled.

Backups and extraction blocked

OS-level controls keep KyPost data out of device backups, cloud backups, and forensic pulls.

Screen-capture protection

Mail, PGP, and security screens are excluded from recordings and screen sharing within each OS's limits.

Costly password guessing

Three wrong passwords lock the account for 15 minutes. Optional self-hosted proof-of-work makes every attempt consume CPU.

Number-matched MFA

Push MFA uses number matching instead of one-tap approval, reducing the risk of MFA-fatigue attacks.

Your own identity provider

Sign in through any OpenID Connect provider — authorization code with PKCE, ID tokens checked against the issuer's JWKS, and accounts claimed by the provider's subject rather than by a username someone else could register. KySignOn is a one-click preset; Authentik and Keycloak have their admin-group claims mapped.

Updates you asked for

The container reports a new release and emails the admin, but never controls Docker on its host. The host-side updater verifies the image's build attestation before it applies anything and rolls back on a failed health check. A daily timer exists, and it is opt-in.

Adversarial reviews

Every repository receives repeated whole-project reviews by an AI adversary. Public fixes include regression tests that fail if the bug returns.

The device protections have landed on Android, Mac & iOS, and Linux, each using the strongest primitive its platform offers — so the details differ where the OS does. The sign-in defences live on your server and cover every client at once.

Make it yours

Categories, rules, and 15 themes — shared everywhere

Every client speaks the same theme language, so the way you sort mail and the way it looks travels with you.

Categories

Unread mail is auto-labeled by a local AI model and sorted into keyword tabs — no manual filing.

Rules

Tune the classifier's prompt per user to change what counts as which category, right from the web UI.

Themes

15 palettes, from Patina Ky to Cyber Punk, defined once and shared byte-for-byte across web, Android, Mac, iOS, and Linux.

Filtering

Inbox rules run as an expanded Sieve script on your own server — write a condition once and every client sees the same filtered result.

Notifications

Pick your delivery path per device: Firebase, APNs, or UnifiedPush for instant push, or plain client pull if you'd rather trade a little immediacy for a lot more privacy. Either way the alert is generic unless you opt into previews.

Contacts

Manage your contacts from any client, synced across web, Android, Mac, iOS, and Linux — and over CardDAV to anything else that speaks it.
